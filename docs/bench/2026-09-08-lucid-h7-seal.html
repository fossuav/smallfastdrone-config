Bench session · 7–8 September 2026

Sealing a drone, and getting back out again

One TBS_LUCID_H7, taken through the two operations the security design had only ever argued about: sealing it against readout, and unsealing it again. Three claims that existed as reasoning went onto silicon. One of them was wrong. Nine defects fell out, and every single one sat in the handover between steps rather than in any of the cryptography.

What was owed

Three things had been designed, built and written down, and none had met a board. Each had been recorded honestly as reasoning rather than measurement, which is what made it obvious they were still owed.

A signed grant works on a sealed drone

The claim decision 42 rests on: a drone whose owner key is lost can be re-pointed by a permission SFD signs, without erasing the airframe. Written off as safe because “the grant path never consults the seal”.

Was not proven

The DFU unlock works on protected silicon

DfuClient.readUnprotect() waits a poll timeout and then requires the status read to fail — success looks like a stall. Never run against a board that was actually read-protected; a Python mirror of it got the reading backwards in September.

Was not proven

The exit ceremony returns a drone intact

Run once before, but on a drone with no owner key and no grant counter. Both exist now, and both live in the sector the exit erases.

Partly

The sequence

Drawn as a sequence because it is one. Step 4 is only meaningful after step 3, and step 5 cannot be undone without steps 6 and 7 — the ordering is the security property, not a workflow.

1

Claimed by presence, unsealed

SET_OWNER_KEY with a bench owner key, authorised by nothing but standing at the drone. Re-claimed a second time to a different key, which an unsealed drone is meant to allow.

2

Ownership grants, five ways

A permission signed offline by SFD’s firmware key, verified by the drone against the key already in its bootloader. Applied; replayed and refused as superseded; refused for another drone; refused when signed by an untrusted key; and a newer counter accepted, rotating the owner.

3

Sealed

BRD_OPTIONS 1 → 1025, raising readout protection at the next boot. One way: the firmware never lowers it.

4

The claim that was wrong

A bare re-claim was refused — already claimed and sealed — which is correct. The grant was refused too, which is not. apply_owner_grant() passed every check and then called set_owner_key(), where the presence rule lives one call deeper than anyone had looked. Fixed, reflashed onto the sealed board, and re-run: accepted, owner changed, replay still refused.

5

Unlocked, and everything with it

DFU read-unprotect through the tool’s own DfuClient, in a browser. The chip mass-erased — firmware, bootloader, identity, owner key and counter — and left the USB bus entirely. That erase is the proof: it happens only on a real RDP 1 → 0 transition.

6

Reflashed from blank

A full _with_bl.hex over DFU, because the erase took the bootloader too. The board came back as the SFD fork built vanilla — unsigned, no key region, no identity region, nothing to seal.

7

Settings back

Fourteen of fourteen, none missing, none differing. BRD_OPTIONS returned as 1 where the drone had held 1025: the backup strips the seal request, so a drone wiped in order to unseal it does not quietly ask to be sealed again on the way back.

A drone survives the round trip

Sealed, wiped, reflashed and restored, with an owner key and grant counter in the erased sector for the first time. 14/14 settings, seal request stripped.

On hardware

A secure command reply is composed and then discarded

Zero replies in forty seconds — nearly three times the client’s allowance — while heartbeats carried on. Firmware instrumentation showed it reaching the send with ACCEPTED. After the fix: every time, at 1.85 s.

On hardware

Nine defects, none of them in the cryptography

That is the finding worth carrying forward. The key agreements, the signatures and the envelopes all behaved. What failed, repeatedly, was the seam between one step and the next — and every one of these sits in a path the simulator cannot reach.

A reply the transmit buffer silently dropped

comm_send_lock() discards a message that will not fit, by design. A 220-byte reply follows a flash write that fills the buffer; a 54-byte statustext fits, and is retried besides — which is why the instrumentation looked like the reply had gone out. Two write-once operations reported failure after succeeding.

Firmware

The same, for a bootloader flash

“Flash OK” and then no COMMAND_ACK in forty-five seconds, on the one command a ground station most needs to know succeeded.

Firmware

A low battery blamed for a failed update

The explanation took the last statustext to arrive, and arming checks narrate on their own schedule. An update that had succeeded was reported as failing because the battery was low.

Tool

A grant refused by the rule beneath it

The one this session was for. Reasoning said the path never consults the seal; it did, one call deeper.

Firmware

Two senders, one writer

WritableStream is already locked on connect. A comment asserted this could not happen because the only sender “serialises by construction”; nothing enforced it. The end-to-end suite runs over a WebSocket, which has no such lock — so the assumption could sit there being wrong indefinitely.

Tool

A file dialog with nothing in it

accept=".hex" hid every other file, so choosing the recovery image could fail with no name, no error, and a disabled button explaining neither.

Tool

“Keep it plugged in”

The wipe takes the board off the USB bus — that is how it proves it worked. The wizard polled thirty seconds and then gave the one instruction guaranteed not to work, at the point where the drone is in its most broken state.

Tool

An FTP handshake against a drone still waking up

A drone brings its filesystem up well after it answers heartbeats, and widest on a first boot after a mass erase — exactly when the restore runs. One 1500 ms attempt, no retry.

Tool

A backup that asked to be sealed

BRD_OPTIONS carries the seal request, so the file taken before the exit held it. Inert on what the wipe leaves behind, and a landmine the day that drone is flashed with SFD firmware again. Found by reading a real backup off the sealed board.

What is still not proven

The ceremony cannot finish what it starts

runExitCeremony() begins by reading settings over MAVLink, and a wiped drone has none — so a failure after the erase leaves a board the wizard can only describe. The Firmware page finished the job here, by hand.

Open

The ack fix, under a real stall

Observed working during the bootloader update in this session — a genuine erase-and-write — which is the stall it was written for. The earlier record left this owed; it is owed no longer.

Now proven

The drone did not come back to upstream ArduPilot

It came back to the SFD fork built vanilla — unsigned, no secure regions — because that is what the recovery image was built from. Functionally an exited drone and the GPL position holds, but it is not the artefact the September run used.

Noted

The thread

September’s record ended by saying what the hardware had proved and what it had not. This one ends by saying where the faults were: not one of the nine was in a key agreement, a signature or an envelope. They were in what happens between steps — a reply that does not fit a buffer, a board that leaves the bus, a dialog with nothing in it, a drone that is not awake yet.

All nine sit in paths a simulator structurally cannot reach. The clearest is the writer lock: the end-to-end suite runs over a WebSocket, which has no writer to contend for, so a comment asserting the contention could not happen was safe from every test in the repository. That is what bench time buys, and it is not the cryptography.
