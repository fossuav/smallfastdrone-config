Bench session · 4–5 September 2026

Taking a Lucid H7 all the way in, and all the way back out

Two days on the bench with one TBS_LUCID_H7. It went from stock ArduPilot to signed SmallFastDrone firmware, gained a secure bootloader and its own cryptographic identity, was sealed against readout, then wiped and returned to vanilla ArduPilot with its settings intact. Nine defects fell out along the way. This is the record of what the hardware actually proved — and, just as deliberately, what it didn’t.

How to read this

Every result below carries one of three marks. The distinction is the point of the document: a great deal was proven on silicon, some was proven only against a reference implementation, and a few things could not be proven at all without destroying something we needed. Conflating those three is how a security claim quietly becomes untrue.

The path through

The upgrade is a genuine one-way sequence — each step changes what the next one can do, and step 5 cannot be undone without step 6. That ordering is a security property, not a workflow convenience, which is why it is drawn as a sequence and nothing else here is.

1

Reach the board at all

A bench harness that owns the serial port and presents it as bytes, so protocol code can be driven without a browser. It runs under Windows Python by necessity: attaching the board to Linux via usbipd works until the board reboots, at which point it re-enumerates, the attachment is silently dropped and the device never returns — killing the one test that most needs real hardware.

test/bench/ · com-pipe.py, serial-link.ts, serial-bridge.ts
2

Signed firmware over the existing bootloader

1,672,284 bytes written and verified in 25.8 s by the tool’s own BootloaderClient. The documented route for this was DFU; the serial route turned out to work and needs no drivers, which is the difference between “press a button” and “install a driver” for anyone upgrading a working drone.

3

The secure bootloader, from the firmware’s own ROMFS

MAV_CMD_FLASH_BOOTLOADER, added to the tool for this. The board afterwards reports itself as TBS_LUCID_H7-Secure-BL-v10 — which, it turns out, is the only non-destructive way to know a secure bootloader is installed at all.

4

An identity, made by the drone

The drone drew an X25519 key from its own hardware RNG, kept the private half in the bootloader sector and returned the public half. A second attempt was DENIED; a reboot returned the same key, proving it is in flash and not RAM.

5

Sealed

BRD_OPTIONS 1 → 1025, raising readout protection at the next boot. Everything else about the drone carried on unchanged: the encrypted applet still ran, the identity still read back. One way, short of erasing the chip.

6

Out again, whole

Settings saved, readout protection dropped, chip mass-erased — firmware, bootloader and identity together — stock image flashed over DFU, settings restored. The board came back as ordinary ArduCopter 4.7.1, which is exactly what an escape hatch should deliver.

What the hardware settled

Sealing genuinely raises readout protection

The tool cannot read the chip’s protection level — there is no MAVLink representation of it, and the firmware’s own call is a no-op once set. So the seal’s effect was unverifiable by design. The erase settled it: the chip mass-erased and vanished from the bus, which only happens on an RDP 1→0 transition. Had the seal been a no-op, the command would have been refused and the firmware would still have been there.

On hardware

A script encrypted for one drone runs on that drone only

An applet encrypted to this board’s identity decrypted and ran; an identical one encrypted to a different identity was refused. This is the capability the entire identity arc exists to deliver, and it now works end to end.

On hardware

Identity survives a firmware update, and write-once holds

Reflashing firmware left the key untouched — it lives in the bootloader sector, as designed. A second GENERATE_IDENTITY came back DENIED, so write-once is enforced in firmware rather than merely intended.

On hardware

The exit ceremony returns a working drone

Backup, unlock, erase, reinstall, restore. planRestore reported honestly under real conditions: one parameter missing because the stock firmware doesn’t have it, and four flagged as unrevertable because stock enables a second IMU the SFD build did not.

On hardware

The .lxa v2 key agreement

v1 encrypted with a bootloader public key used as a symmetric key — obfuscation, not encryption, since that key sits in every bootloader. v2 agrees a key by X25519 against the drone’s identity. Round-tripped against pymonocypher at the firmware’s own header offsets before it ever reached the board.

Offline

What broke, and where it came from

Eight findings: five fixed in the session, three logged with enough detail to act on. One of the five — the frozen metadata — was three separate bugs stacked behind each other. Notably, none was caught by the unit suite; every one needed either real hardware or a look at a rendered page.

fixed · found on hardware

A confident wrong answer

Toggle a setting, reboot, and the drone reported it off while actually holding it on. After a reboot the parameter reload stalls — the board answers heartbeats a half-second in but cannot stream 1,242 parameters until roughly four — and no caller checked whether the reload had worked. All seven reboot flows shared the bug. SITL never exposed it because its restart is differently shaped.

fixed · found by looking at the screen

The SFD detector never fired

The connect card said “ArduPilot” on a drone whose banner reads SmallFastDrone V4.7.0. The sniffer matched a version suffix; the product build replaces the vehicle name outright. So it had never worked on the board that most obviously is a SmallFastDrone.

fixed · three separate bugs

Parameter metadata was frozen

The generator had been unable to run across two submodule bumps. The cause was a missing blank line between two documentation blocks; the regeneration then exposed a parameter documented under a name no drone reports; and a group-name collision in the JSON emitter was silently dropping whole libraries. Board coverage went from 1,274/1,282 to 1,282/1,282.

fixed · found during the exit ceremony

Gyro calibration is not a setting

Four of seven restored values “failed” verification. Rebooting twice with no writes at all showed them moving on their own: the drone re-measures gyro offsets every boot. They are measurements wearing the shape of settings, and saving them promises something a restore cannot keep.

fixed · latent, would have bitten later

An unreachable timeout

A test budgeted 135 s across its assertions but never raised the 30 s default, so its final wait could never be reached. It passed on SITL only because everything there is fast. Fixing it is what let the real bug above surface.

logged

Connecting rewrites the drone

The tool asks for telemetry on connect; ArduPilot persists that as nine MAV* parameters. So merely plugging in permanently alters the drone — and those nine then ride along in every settings backup, a document whose premise is that it holds only what the operator changed.

logged

An errno in front of an operator

A drone with no memory card fails every file write with FTP CREATE_DIRECTORY failed: FailErrno (errno 28). Every Lua wizard sits on that path. Distinguishing “no card” from “broken” is cheap: the virtual filesystem lists fine on a card-less board.

logged

Parameter streaming is lossy under load

With a Lua applet running, the parameter stream drops one roughly half the time where it was clean on day one. Not a correctness bug — but it makes the reload retry added for the first finding load-bearing rather than precautionary.

The measurement that shaped a fix

Rather than guess a settle delay for the post-reboot reload, the board was asked directly: reboot, wait a fixed interval, then request the full parameter set. One reboot per interval, so the attempts could not perturb each other.

A single-parameter read was tested as a cheaper readiness signal and answered in 133 ms — while the full request still stalled. So neither a heartbeat nor a successful read means ready, and there is no cheap signal to wait on. That is why the fix is a retry against a budget rather than a tuned delay: the constant is a latency optimisation, and correctness rests on the retry.

What was not proven, and why

Four claims the bench could not close

The WebUSB DFU client. The unlock and reflash were driven through pyusb and STM32CubeProgrammer, because DfuClient only runs in a browser. What is proven is that the escape hatch works, not that our implementation of it does. Worth noting the shipped code is the more careful of the two: the bench mirror treated a status read as needing to succeed, when that read failing is the success.

The production serial transport. Everything reached the board over a WebSocket bridge, so WebSerialTransport itself is still exercised only by a human in Chrome.

Where a foreign script is refused. It is definitely refused. But instrumentation at the load function’s first line never reached the ground station and could not be reconciled, so “refused before any cipher work” is what the source says rather than what was observed. The documentation now says so.

The downgrade refusal. It is inert on any board whose keys are intact, so seeing it work means deliberately emptying a keyed board’s keys.

What I would carry forward

Four times during this session I diagnosed a problem from reading code, and four times I was wrong — once expensively, with a “fix” that dropped 320 parameters before being reverted. What settled it each time was cheap instrumentation that had been available from the start: a --verbose flag, a print at a branch, reading a value back off the board. The pattern is clear enough to name: when a theory is about what code does, measure it before acting on it.

The second thing worth keeping is the habit that shaped this document. Several results here are weaker than they could have been made to sound. A seal that is “set” is not a seal that is proven raised; a script that is “refused” is not a script refused at the cheap check. Recording that difference costs a sentence and is the only thing that makes the strong claims worth anything.
